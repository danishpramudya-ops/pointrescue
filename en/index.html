01 — Problem

Search operations always happen exactly where technology least wants to be: forest, mountain slope, river valley, ground a disaster has just flattened. And that is precisely where the cell towers have come down, the power is out, and every app that demands the internet stops being of any use.

01

Areas with no signal

A blank spot is not the exception — it is a search-and-rescue team's daily working ground. Any device that needs a cellular signal just to open a map stops working at exactly the moment it matters most.

02

Teame that cannot be negotiated

A victim's chance of survival drops sharply with every hour. Every hour spent sweeping the wrong area never comes back — and uncoordinated sweeping is the most common reason those hours are lost.

03

Coordination in the blind

With no shared data channel, the command post does not know where its teams are and the teams do not know what other teams have already covered. Reports travel by voice radio, one at a time, and vanish the moment the conversation ends.

Point Rescue starts from a single design decision: never depend on the infrastructure most likely to be destroyed alongside everything else.

Pull & Play

Pull, and the unit switches on.

On the victim node, a magnetic reed switch is held by a magnet on a pull tab. While the magnet stays in place the unit sleeps and saves power. Pull the tab and the magnet moves away: the unit switches on at once and starts transmitting its coordinates — no menu, no app, no setup.

03 — Approach

A network the team carries in with them.

01

No carrier

The moment it is switched on, every unit forms an independent radio network with the other units around it. No SIM card, no registration, no server. Each unit is at once sender, receiver and relay.

  • LoRa 433 MHz · Net ID PR01
  • Sync word 0x24
  • 64-byte maximum payload
02

Controlled flooding

A packet that arrives is forwarded again by other nodes, so effective range reaches far beyond what a single radio can cover. That forwarding is deliberately controlled so the air does not clog with copies of the same message.

  • 1–4 hop budget per packet type
  • Random 400–700 ms delay
  • Duplicate suppression by sequence number
03

Shared picture

The same data reaches two places: the operator dashboard at the command post and the Android app in a rescuer's hand. Nobody has to walk back to the post just to find out what is happening.

  • Tactical map, trails, compass
  • Two-way mission messaging
  • All processing on the device
04

Holding up when things fail

Every technical decision comes from one question: what happens if this part does not work? The map has three fallback levels, the last valid position is held, and a link can be proven all the way to the command post.

  • Three-tier map fallback
  • Last valid position held
  • Tiered link proof

04 — How it works

Three roles, one network.

The hardware is identical; the only difference is the role the firmware gives it. All three speak the same protocol, so any unit can relay for any other with no extra configuration.

K victim · S SAR team · G command-post gateway

VICTIM node

Carried by a victim, or left at the point where one is found

Broadcasts its identity and coordinates at intervals. One button to arm it — no screen, no menu, still working from inside a bag.

SAR node

Carried by personnel in the field

As well as broadcasting, the SAR node bridges over Bluetooth to the Android app: packets from the network reach the screen, messages from the screen reach the network.

GATEWAY node

Installed at the command post

Connects to the post's computer over USB and pours all network traffic into the operator dashboard.

  1. Hop budget. Every packet carries a hopallowance. A heartbeat gets one hop, a mission message up to four. When the budget runs out, the packet stops.
  2. Random delay. Before forwarding, a node waits a random 400–700 ms. Two nodes that hear the same packet will not re-send it at exactly the same instant.
  3. Duplicate suppression. Each node records the last sequence number from every sender. A copy that arrives later by a different path is discarded.

Four things that must not be concluded from this data

The post location for the last two sessions is an estimate

The gateway has no GPS. On 30 July the location was measured by placing a GPS-equipped node at the post; on 14 and 18 August it was taken from the strongest-RSSI packet. Those estimates land 8 m and 16 m from the measured point — comparable to the GPS fix error itself, but do not call the distance axis of those two sessions a measurement.

PDR here is a per-node aggregate

One node, one sequence counter for every packet type. Do not claim an “SOS PDR of X per cent” from this data.

Range is not the device's maximum

The figure is the farthest distance that happened to be tested in that session. The session ended because the person stopped walking, not because the signal ran out. Do not quote this number as a range specification.

These sessions are not a like-for-like comparison

Node count, recording length and terrain all differ; the 24 July session even used firmware with a different format, and two sessions have no recorded date. Do not read them as “steadily getting better”.

Nodes reboot on their own in every session

The packet sequence number returns to the start every time a node reboots — 22 times across the eight sessions, right from the earliest recording. This data proves it happens, but not why: a voltage drop, a program crash and a loose cable leave exactly the same trace in the gateway log. The firmware now prints a [BOOT] line carrying the reboot reason according to the ESP32 itself.

What has been proven

The radio parameters are identical across all three roles — SF10, BW 125 kHz, CR 4/5, sync word 0x24 — and that is what makes the eight sessions comparable at all. In the multi-hop test the gateway stayed put, one node walked away and one node stood in the middle: eight packets are proven to have arrived via a relay, the farthest at 391 m, while the farthest recorded direct link was 1,891 m.

The numbers are computed by tools/anchorpulse.py from the gateway's serial recording, then assembled into validasi/data.js by tools/susun_validasi.py. This page presents them; it does not recompute them.

09 — In hand

A command post that walks with you.

The Android app connects to a SAR node over Bluetooth Low Energy. Five working areas — Dashboard, Map, Nodes, Messages, More — and all of them function without the internet. What is running on the phone beside this is the app itself, built for the web from the same Flutter code: tap the tab bar, pan the map, open a node's detail.
